# Fine-Tuning Small Open-Weight LLMs (1B–14B) on Consumer Hardware for Structured Output and Tool-Calling (2026 methodology)

## LoRA/QLoRA feasibility and VRAM budgets on consumer GPUs; full fine-tuning alternative

### Takeaway
QLoRA remains the practical default for 3B–14B models on single consumer GPUs: roughly 6–10GB for 3B, 8–14GB for 7B/8B, and 10–17GB for 14B depending on rank/batch/sequence length, making 12GB cards workable for 7B and 16GB+ a safer target for 14B. Full fine-tuning is realistic only for 1B–3B models and needs materially more headroom (16–24GB+), with 7B-class full fine-tunes requiring CPU/RAM offload and much slower throughput on a single consumer card.

### Cited Findings
- A 2026 GPU-requirements guide (Spheron) reports QLoRA (4-bit NF4) VRAM at roughly 8GB for 7B/8B models and roughly 14GB for 14B models, and lists an RTX 5090 (32GB) as its recommended minimum card for comfortable headroom — [Spheron 2026 guide](https://www.spheron.network/blog/gpu-vram-requirements-fine-tune-llm-2026/)
- A practitioner-tested guide (InsiderLLM) reports lower, more granular figures: ~6–7GB for a 7B model at LoRA rank 16/batch 1, ~8–9GB at rank 16/batch 2, ~9–10GB at rank 32/batch 2; and for a 14B model (Qwen 2.5), ~8.5–10GB, ~12–13GB, and ~14GB under the same three settings, with 12GB described as a "tight minimum" for 14B and 16GB as "comfortable" — [InsiderLLM: LoRA training on consumer hardware](https://insiderllm.com/guides/lora-training-consumer-hardware/)
- Modal's September 2024 VRAM guide (older, pre-2025 tooling) gives ~9GB for a 7B QLoRA fine-tune and ~17GB for 13B — [Modal: How much VRAM for fine-tuning](https://modal.com/blog/how-much-vram-need-fine-tuning)
- A Hugging Face forum report of an actual QLoRA run on a 3B model (open_llama_3b_v2, max seq len 512, paged 8-bit optimizer, SFTTrainer) measured peak GPU memory around 10GB even though the base model's on-disk footprint is only ~2.5GB — [HF forum: QLoRA 3B model 10GB](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/qlora-memory-requirement-with-3b-model-loads-gpu-with-10gb-of-memory-with-4bit-quantization/66336)
- The same forum thread area indicates 7B QLoRA can fit on an 8GB-class GPU with micro-batch size 1 and gradient accumulation, implying 3B is workable on 8GB with similar memory-saving settings (short sequences, micro-batch 1, gradient checkpointing) — [HF forum: VRAM for small models on a laptop](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/how-much-vram-do-you-realistically-need-to-fine-tune-small-models-on-a-laptop/180697)
- On full fine-tuning feasibility: a library's own measured benchmark (`backpropagate`) reports a true full fine-tune of a 3B model peaking at ~22GiB on a 32GB card, and states that a 16GB GPU tier is sufficient for full fine-tuning of ~3B models (e.g., Llama-3.2-1B/3B), while 7B-class full fine-tuning needs host-RAM offload and runs much slower — [PyPI: backpropagate 1.7.2 docs](https://pypi.org/project/backpropagate/1.7.2/)
- A GPU-selection guide (Jarvislabs) estimates full fine-tuning of 1B–3B models at 24–40GB VRAM (AdamW + mixed precision + gradient checkpointing), pointing to a 24GB card (e.g., RTX 4090) as the practical minimum, and notes that without gradient checkpointing activation memory can roughly double — [Jarvislabs: Best GPU for fine-tuning LLMs](https://jarvislabs.ai/ai-faqs/best-gpu-for-fine-tuning-llms)
- The Kaitchup (Benjamin Marie) states that the small size of Llama 3.2 1B/3B "even allows for full fine-tuning on a consumer GPU with 24GB of memory" (comparison of LoRA/QLoRA/full fine-tuning; full article paywalled) — [Kaitchup: Fine-tuning Meta's Llama 3.2 1B/3B](https://kaitchup.substack.com/p/fine-tuning-metas-llama-32-1b-3b)
- The original LoRA paper (Hu et al., arXiv 2106.09685, ICLR) reports that compared to fully fine-tuning GPT-3 175B with Adam, LoRA cuts trainable parameters by ~10,000x and reduces GPU memory requirement by roughly 3x — [arXiv 2106.09685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685); secondary summary — [IBM: What is LoRA](https://www.ibm.com/topics/lora)
- The original QLoRA paper (Dettmers et al., NeurIPS 2023, arXiv 2305.14314) demonstrates fine-tuning a 65B-parameter model on a single 48GB GPU while preserving full 16-bit fine-tuning task performance, via 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization of the frozen base model plus double quantization and paged optimizers (the latter uses NVIDIA unified memory to absorb gradient-checkpointing memory spikes and avoid OOM) — [NeurIPS 2023 proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/1feb87871436031bdc0f2beaa62a049b-Abstract.html)
- QLoRA's Guanaco model family reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT's performance on the Vicuna benchmark after ~24 hours of fine-tuning on a single GPU; NF4 consistently outperforms FP4 by roughly one percentage point of MMLU accuracy in the same paper's ablations — [NeurIPS 2023 proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/1feb87871436031bdc0f2beaa62a049b-Abstract.html); [Liner review summary](https://liner.com/review/qlora-efficient-finetuning-quantized-llms)

### Inferences
- For the vyapaar-agent use case (3B–7B class model, closed-schema Decision enum output), QLoRA on a single 12–16GB consumer GPU (e.g., RTX 4060 Ti 16GB, RTX 3060 12GB, RTX 4070 Ti 12-16GB) is well within reported envelopes across multiple independent sources, with margin for longer context (market+portfolio state) by using gradient checkpointing and micro-batch 1 with gradient accumulation.
- If a 14B model is desired for either task, a 16GB card is the realistic floor per InsiderLLM's measured figures, but 24GB (e.g., RTX 4090) gives comfortable margin matching Spheron's more conservative estimate.
- Full fine-tuning is only worth considering for the 1B–3B tier, and even then needs ~16–24GB VRAM depending on sequence length/checkpointing settings — i.e., roughly the same or more hardware than QLoRA needs for a 7B model. Given the narrow-schema nature of both target tasks, QLoRA/LoRA on a slightly larger base model is likely a better quality/cost tradeoff than full fine-tuning a smaller one, though no source directly benchmarks this tradeoff for closed-schema tasks specifically (gap).

### Gaps
- No controlled, apples-to-apples 2026 benchmark across 8GB/12GB/16GB/24GB tiers for the exact 3B/7B/14B triple was found; all figures above are drawn from different sources with different rank/batch/sequence settings and should be treated as a plausible range, not a precise spec.
- No source gives full fine-tuning throughput (tokens/sec or wall-clock) on a single consumer GPU without offload; only offloaded (slower) full fine-tuning timings were mentioned.

## Best tools/frameworks for consumer-hardware-constrained fine-tuning

### Takeaway
Unsloth is the clear leader for single-consumer-GPU LoRA/QLoRA efficiency (custom kernels, claimed up to ~70% VRAM reduction and multi-x speedups), Axolotl is the config-driven choice for broader model/task coverage (including vision-language models) at some VRAM/speed cost, and LLaMA-Factory offers the broadest model support plus a web UI but depends on enabling an Unsloth backend to match Unsloth's speed. MLX is the Apple Silicon equivalent, using unified memory instead of a fixed VRAM pool.

### Cited Findings
- In an independently-reported benchmark (repeated across multiple sources, possibly tracing to one original test) of Llama-3.1 8B QLoRA fine-tuning on an A100 40GB, 2 epochs, 512-token sequences: Unsloth completed in ~3.2 hours, Axolotl in ~5.8 hours, LLaMA-Factory-with-Unsloth-backend in ~3.4 hours, and LLaMA-Factory without that backend in ~5 hours — [TheAIEngineer Substack](https://theaiengineer.substack.com/p/unsloth-vs-axolotl-vs-llama-factory); same figures also reported by [Spheron: Axolotl vs Unsloth vs TorchTune](https://www.spheron.network/blog/axolotl-vs-unsloth-vs-torchtune/)
- One source reports Unsloth fine-tuning Qwen3 30B-A3B (MoE, 3 active experts) at ~12x the speed of standard PyTorch while fitting in 17.5GB VRAM; another source claims Unsloth enables fine-tuning Llama-3.1 20B-class models on 24GB with QLoRA where standard implementations would OOM — [TheAIEngineer Substack](https://theaiengineer.substack.com/p/unsloth-vs-axolotl-vs-llama-factory)
- Framework-level tradeoffs reported: Unsloth's custom kernels give it the edge on raw single-GPU efficiency but it is single-GPU-focused; Axolotl is config-driven and production-pipeline oriented, and supports vision-language model fine-tuning (Qwen2-VL, LLaVA) as first-class, which Unsloth lacks; LLaMA-Factory has the broadest model support and a web UI, with speed heavily dependent on whether the Unsloth backend is enabled — [TheAIEngineer Substack](https://theaiengineer.substack.com/p/unsloth-vs-axolotl-vs-llama-factory)
- For Apple Silicon: MLX (`mlx-lm`) supports LoRA, QLoRA, DoRA, and full fine-tuning (`--fine-tune-type full/dora`). Its unified memory architecture means the entire RAM pool is available for training rather than a fixed VRAM ceiling, but training is bandwidth-constrained so it runs slower per step than a discrete GPU — [MLX-LoRA-Studio docs](https://goekdeniz-guelmez.github.io/MLX-LoRA-Studio/Full-Fine-Tuning); [DZone MLX-LM guide](https://dzone.com/articles/fine-tuning-llms-locally-using-mlx-lm-guide)
- Practical MLX sizing guidance: a 16GB Mac is suited to QLoRA (not LoRA) on an 8B-class model; a 32GB Mac can run LoRA on an 8B model with settings like `--batch-size 2 --num-layers 16 --mask-prompt`; a 16GB config example uses `--batch-size 2 --num-layers 8 --rank 4` — [InsiderLLM: Fine-tuning on Mac with MLX](https://insiderllm.com/guides/fine-tuning-mac-lora-mlx/)

### Inferences
- For a single RTX/consumer NVIDIA GPU workflow, Unsloth is the strongest default starting point purely on VRAM-efficiency and speed grounds for both target tasks (structured-output and tool-calling fine-tunes on 3B–14B models), with Axolotl as the fallback if a model/architecture isn't yet supported by Unsloth or if a config-driven, more "production pipeline" style is preferred.
- On Apple Silicon, MLX with QLoRA is the right choice for 7B–8B class models on 16GB unified-memory machines; LoRA/full fine-tuning becomes viable only at 32GB+ unified memory.

### Gaps
- No 2026-specific, independently-verified (non-marketing) benchmark comparing all of Unsloth/Axolotl/LLaMA-Factory/HF PEFT+TRL on the exact same consumer GPU (e.g., a single RTX 4090 or 3090) was found — the available numbers are from an A100 40GB, which is not consumer hardware, so consumer-GPU speed deltas between frameworks are inferred, not directly measured.
- No source directly addresses HF PEFT/TRL's standalone (non-Unsloth-accelerated) VRAM/speed figures on consumer GPUs for comparison.

## Constrained decoding vs. fine-tuning for closed-schema structured output

### Takeaway
Constrained/grammar-based decoding (GBNF in llama.cpp, Outlines, Guidance, vLLM's structured-output backends such as XGrammar) is mature enough in 2026 to guarantee near-100% syntactic/schema validity on closed schemas, but it does not guarantee semantic or value-level correctness — several sources report constrained decoding can pick a schema-valid but wrong value, and in at least one benchmark constrained decoding scored lower on accuracy than free-form parsing. The strongest evidence (SLOT paper) is that fine-tuning plus constrained decoding together produce the best outcomes, not either alone.

### Cited Findings
- vLLM uses constrained decoding by masking invalid tokens' logits to negative infinity before sampling, which removes any chance of generating a non-conforming token; XGrammar is reported (uncorroborated against primary vLLM docs) to be vLLM's default structured-output backend, claimed to deliver up to 100x speedup over naive grammar-constrained methods — [theneuralbase: vLLM structured output reliability](https://theneuralbase.com/vllm/learn/intermediate/structured-output-reliability/)
- A documented vLLM pitfall: setting `guided_decoding` with `type: 'json_object'` but omitting the actual `json_schema` dict produces syntactically valid JSON that is NOT constrained to the intended structure — [tianpan.co: structured outputs in production](https://tianpan.co/blog/2025/10/29/structured-outputs-llm-production)
- A third-party test (GitHub project "grammarexact") reports llama.cpp's GBNF sampler matches an independent Earley-parser oracle at whole-codepoint states across tested grammars, but also reports a soundness bug where alternations with mixed-length character classes can allow overlong, invalid UTF-8 (attributed to a specific function in `llama-grammar.cpp`, tested on build b9760 with a Qwen2.5 tokenizer) — unverified against the upstream repo — [grammarexact GitHub project, via search summary]
- A 2026 practitioner report (Tian Pan) describes cutting post-processing errors from 32% to 0.4% by switching to constrained decoding, but on the same test set, constrained output scored 91.37% accuracy vs. 93.63% for free-form parsing — including a concrete failure case where a constrained-decoded output returned a plausible but wrong numeric value (1 instead of 0.46) — [tianpan.co: grammar-constrained generation output reliability](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/16/grammar-constrained-generation-output-reliability)
- A RANLP 2025 study found base (non-instruction-tuned) models often benefit from constrained decoding (more precise outputs), while instruction-tuned models frequently show performance degradation on generation tasks under constraints — log-probability analysis suggests constraints force the model away from its natural preferred patterns into lower-confidence structured alternatives — [ACL Anthology, RANLP 2025 preview](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2025.ranlp-1.124)
- An EMNLP-cited result (via secondary blog, not independently verified against the original paper) reports strict format constraints degraded reasoning accuracy by up to 27 percentage points on math benchmarks, with the suggested mitigation being free-form reasoning followed by constrained final-answer output — [tianpan.co, cited secondhand]
- The SLOT paper (arXiv 2505.04016) reports that a fine-tuned Mistral-7B combined with constrained decoding reaches near-perfect schema accuracy (99.5%) and content similarity (94.0%), describing this combination as a "powerful synergy" — note "content similarity" is a benchmark metric, not a strict per-field correctness measure — [arXiv 2505.04016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04016)
- A separate grammar-constrained decoding paper (arXiv 2305.13971) found that grammar-constrained LMs can substantially outperform unconstrained LMs, and in some cases even outperform task-specific fine-tuned models — i.e., constrained decoding alone on a base model can beat a fine-tuned model without constraints on some structured NLP tasks — [arXiv 2305.13971](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.13971)
- Outlines (maintained by .txt/dottxt, originally from Normal Computing) runs the same constrained-generation code against OpenAI, Ollama, and vLLM backends, with its core state-machine logic ported to Rust (`outlines-core`) for speed; a third-party aggregator lists a March 2026 release (v1.2.12), unconfirmed against the official repo — [KDnuggets: Outlines](https://www.kdnuggets.com/structured-language-model-generation-with-outlines); [dottxt-ai docs](https://dottxt-ai.github.io/outlines/main)
- Guidance (github.com/guidance-ai/guidance, MIT license, originated at Microsoft Research) supports interleaving free text with `select`, regex, context-free-grammar, and JSON-schema constraints, and is reported as faster than retry-based structured output because invalid tokens are never sampled in the first place; maintenance-activity claims conflict across sources (one review calls it "less actively maintained than alternatives," another cites a changelog entry dated 2026-05-01) — [AI Tools Atlas review](https://aitoolsatlas.ai/tools/guidance/review)

### Inferences
- For vyapaar's task (a) — closed Rust enum with `deny_unknown_fields`, already passed through a deterministic risk-clamping engine downstream — constrained/grammar decoding at inference time should be adopted regardless of fine-tuning, since it is mature, low-risk, and guarantees the LLM output will at minimum parse into the enum shape (eliminating a whole class of pipeline failures). This is consistent with the project's existing architecture, where the LLM is "never trusted directly, only as a candidate signal" — grammar decoding enforces the *syntactic* half of that safety net for free.
- Fine-tuning remains necessary to improve which enum value / field values are chosen (semantic accuracy), since multiple sources agree constrained decoding guarantees form, not content, and can even produce confidently wrong values. The SLOT paper's result (fine-tuning + constraints together beating either alone) is the best evidence available that both should be used jointly, not substituted for one another.
- Because the downstream risk-clamping engine already treats the LLM output as an untrusted candidate signal, the RANLP 2025 finding that instruction-tuned models can degrade under constraints is a reason to fine-tune on the exact closed schema (rather than relying on a generic instruction-tuned base model + grammar alone), so the model's natural output distribution already aligns with the constrained grammar instead of fighting it.

### Gaps
- No source gives a controlled measurement of fine-tuned-model value accuracy with vs. without constrained decoding on the same closed enum schema — the SLOT and GBNF-paper results are the closest proxies but use different models/tasks/metrics, so the magnitude of fine-tuning's semantic-accuracy contribution specifically for a small closed enum (as opposed to open JSON extraction) is not directly evidenced.
- The llama.cpp GBNF soundness bug report is from an unverified third-party source and could not be confirmed against the upstream llama.cpp issue tracker within this research pass.

## Data requirements: narrow structured-output vs. broader tool-calling fine-tunes

### Takeaway
Published guidance diverges sharply by scale of ambition: narrow, single-schema structured-output or small tool-calling fine-tunes are reported working with a few hundred to a few thousand examples (with one explicit "well under 1,000 may still help" signal from LIMA-style quality-over-quantity results), while broad research-grade tool-calling corpora (ToolACE, APIGen/xlam-function-calling) run from ~26K APIs/dialogs to 60K labeled entries. No source gives a single authoritative number for "N examples needed" — all guidance is practitioner-level and task-dependent.

### Cited Findings
- TinyAgent (function calling at the edge, arXiv 2409.00608) fine-tuned small models with LoRA on ~80,000 training examples for 3 epochs at learning rate 7e-5 — a large, research-scale tool-calling dataset — [arXiv 2409.00608](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.00608)
- APIGen (NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2406.18518) released a verified function-calling dataset of 60,000 entries spanning 3,673 APIs across 21 categories (xlam-function-calling-60k on Hugging Face), generated via a three-stage verification pipeline (format checking, actual function execution, semantic verification) — [NeurIPS 2024 proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/61cce86d180b1184949e58939c4f983d-Abstract.html)
- ToolACE (arXiv 2409.00920) curates an API pool of 26,507 diverse APIs and generates dialogs via multi-agent interplay with a formalized "thinking process"; a total dialogue count was not confirmed in available excerpts (a separate, possibly-different follow-up work is tied to 8,000 synthetic dialogues via "ToolFlow" for SFT on Llama-3.1-8B, but this should not be treated as ToolACE's own dataset size without checking the paper directly) — [ToolACE paper summary](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/5ac1829317c44a1fda91762a)
- A practical tutorial (Nebius AI Studio) fine-tuned on a 10K-example subset of the ~26.5K-dialog ToolACE dataset (1K held for validation) specifically to control training cost, stating this reduced-size subset still shows the benefits of a fine-tuned model — [Nebius: Fine-tuning LLMs with Nebius AI Studio](https://nebius.com/blog/posts/fine-tuning-llms-with-nebius-ai-studio)
- Generic (non-tool-calling-specific) OpenAI community guidance states that ~10 examples is typically too little to see meaningful fine-tuning improvement, and recommends starting with a dataset closer to 30 examples as an absolute floor for any detectable effect — [OpenAI community forum](https://community.openai.com/t/do-i-need-to-improve-my-fine-tuning/719945)
- A vendor practitioner blog suggests 5–10 distinct tools is the "sweet spot" for a first function-calling fine-tuning run (a statement about tool-count scope, not example count) — [Ertas.ai: fine-tuning tool-calling AI agents](https://www.ertas.ai/blog/fine-tuning-tool-calling-ai-agents)
- LIMA (Zhou et al., Meta AI, arXiv 2305.11206) fine-tuned a 65B LLaMA model with standard supervised loss on just 1,000 curated prompt-response pairs (750 sourced from Stack Exchange/wikiHow, 250 hand-written), and human raters found its outputs equal to or better than GPT-4 in 43% of comparisons, better than Bard in 58%, and better than text-davinci-003 in 65% — demonstrating that a small, curated, high-quality dataset can be sufficient for teaching response *format/style* ("superficial alignment hypothesis": most knowledge comes from pretraining; fine-tuning mainly teaches style/format) — [arXiv 2305.11206](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11206)
- LIMA's own limitations section notes it is "not as robust as product-grade models" — an unlucky decoding sample or adversarial prompt can produce a weak response, and building the high-quality curated dataset itself is hard to scale — [arXiv 2305.11206](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11206)

### Inferences
- For vyapaar's task (a) (a single fixed Decision enum over market+portfolio context — a much narrower target than general instruction-following), the LIMA result is a reasonable analogy supporting that a few hundred to ~1,000 carefully curated, diverse examples covering the enum's decision space (including edge cases and class balance) could plausibly suffice to teach the model the target "format and decision style," especially combined with constrained decoding to guarantee syntax regardless. This is an inference, not a directly cited number for closed-enum classification tasks — no source gave a dataset-size figure for a closed-enum fine-tune specifically.
- For task (b) with ~10–30 tools, the Ertas.ai tool-count guidance (5–10 tools) suggests 10–30 tools is somewhat above the reported "sweet spot" for a first run, implying more examples per tool and/or more training iteration may be needed than a minimal function-calling fine-tune; the Nebius 10K-subset result (cut down from 26.5K) is the most directly transferable concrete number available, suggesting low-to-mid thousands of examples is a reasonable starting target rather than the TinyAgent/APIGen tens-of-thousands scale, which looks aimed at building a general-purpose tool-calling foundation capability rather than adapting an existing instruction-tuned model to a fixed internal tool set.

### Gaps
- No source gives a controlled ablation of dataset size specifically for closed-schema/enum classification fine-tuning (as opposed to open-ended JSON extraction or general tool-calling) — this is the single biggest gap relative to vyapaar's task (a).
- No source directly states a recommended example-count range for 10–30 distinct tools specifically; the 5–10 "sweet spot" and the 10K/60K/80K figures bracket this but don't pin it down.
- The ToolACE total dialogue count could not be confirmed from available excerpts.

## Synthetic data generation via teacher-student distillation: best practices and pitfalls

### Takeaway
Best practice is to generate synthetic prompts/distillation data from a frontier "teacher" model, verify outputs against ground truth (schema validation, execution checks, not just LLM-grading), and mix synthetic data with real production-like data rather than relying on synthetic alone; known pitfalls include teacher-error amplification (the student cannot exceed the teacher on tasks the teacher demonstrates poorly), evaluation blind spots when relying only on LLM-graded quality, and model collapse risk if models are trained recursively on their own synthetic outputs.

### Cited Findings
- A 2026 post-training distillation paper found synthetic data helps despite being noisier than real data, and recommends using synthetic data as a "warm-up" stage that broadens the student's exposure, then finishing with distillation on real, teacher-annotated data for the final stage — [arXiv 2606.22942](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22942) (note: this arXiv ID reflects a 2026-dated preprint per the search result; verify numbering against arXiv directly if citing)
- A Microsoft study on Llama 3.1 distillation (arXiv 2410.18588) warns of evaluation pitfalls and recommends task-specific evaluation combining human grading, LLM grading, AND ground-truth accuracy benchmarks — not relying on any single evaluation method — [arXiv 2410.18588](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18588v1)
- A practitioner guide states the student model "can never exceed the teacher on tasks the teacher didn't demonstrate well" — if the teacher struggles with a task, distillation amplifies that same failure in the student — [PremAI: synthetic training data for LLM fine-tuning 2026 guide](https://www.premai.io/blog/how-to-generate-synthetic-training-data-for-llm-fine-tuning-2026-guide/)
- The same guide notes format transfer as a genuine benefit of distillation: if the teacher consistently outputs structured JSON, the student learns to do the same — implying any teacher inconsistency or malformed-output tendency would also transfer to the student (inferred, not directly tested in the source)
- General synthetic-data guidance recommends mixing synthetic data with real data and filtering for quality, and flags "model collapse" risk when models are trained on their own (or same-family) synthetic outputs in a recursive loop, causing progressive divergence from the real data distribution and amplification of artifacts — [theneuralbase: generated synthetic data](https://theneuralbase.com/model-distillation/learn/advanced/generated-synthetic-data/)
- A study on task-specific distillation of visual models (arXiv 2402.11305) found that adding synthetic images as direct supervised labels did not help because the generator was not faithful enough to each class — suggesting synthetic data may be safer to use only within a distillation/soft-label loss rather than as hard ground-truth labels in some settings; relevance to text/structured-output tasks specifically is unconfirmed — [arXiv 2402.11305](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.11305)
- APIGen's verified-generation pipeline (relevant precedent for tool-calling distillation specifically) runs each synthetic data point through three hierarchical verification stages — format checking, actual function execution, and semantic verification — before inclusion in the training set, rather than trusting the generating model's output directly — [NeurIPS 2024 APIGen](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/61cce86d180b1184949e58939c4f983d-Abstract.html)

### Inferences
- For vyapaar's task (a), the APIGen-style "execute and verify" pattern maps directly: generated synthetic (market/portfolio context → Decision enum) examples should be passed through the actual deterministic risk-clamping engine and schema validator before being accepted into the training set, not merely accepted on the teacher's say-so — this guards against the "teacher error amplification" pitfall by using a ground-truth oracle (the deterministic engine) that vyapaar already has, rather than relying purely on LLM-judge quality.
- For task (b) tool-calling, the same execute-and-verify principle applies: since the tools are read-only financial data lookups, synthetic tool-calling examples can be verified by actually executing the generated tool call against a test/sandboxed version of the real API and checking the call is well-typed and semantically sensible, closing the distribution-mismatch gap between synthetic and production inputs that the user flagged as a risk.
- Given LIMA's quality-over-quantity result and the distillation literature's warning about teacher-error amplification, a mixed strategy — a smaller set of real/curated production-like examples (even hand-written) blended with a larger synthetic set generated from frontier-model teachers and filtered through the deterministic engine — is better supported by the cited findings than relying on either pure-synthetic or pure-hand-written data alone.

### Gaps
- No source specifically addresses distribution mismatch between synthetic prompts and real production inputs for structured-output/tool-calling tasks (the user's named concern) — the "model collapse" and format-transfer findings are the closest adjacent evidence, but direct measurement of synthetic-vs-production prompt distribution mismatch was not found in this research pass.
- No source gives a concrete, named teacher/student model pairing recipe (e.g., "use GPT-4-class teacher to generate for a 3B Llama student") with reported accuracy deltas, specifically for closed-schema financial decision output.

## Domain-specific guidance for financial/trading-decision fine-tuning

### Takeaway
Published work on fine-tuning LLMs for trading decisions is dominated by reinforcement-learning-augmented approaches (SFT followed by RL with domain-specific rewards) rather than pure SFT on a closed enum, and at least one recent evaluation explicitly flags "full fine-tuning vs. LoRA/QLoRA for trading agents" as unresolved future work — direct guidance transferable to vyapaar's narrower closed-enum + separate deterministic risk-clamp architecture is sparse in the literature.

### Cited Findings
- Trading-R1 (arXiv 2509.11420) trains with supervised fine-tuning followed by reinforcement learning using a three-stage easy-to-hard curriculum, on a dataset of 100,000 samples spanning 18 months, 14 equities, and five heterogeneous financial data sources; its output space is discretized into five intuitive actions (not exact price predictions), and the RL stage uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO, a PPO variant) with volatility-adjusted rewards — [arXiv 2509.11420 via ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2509.11420)
- A separate RL-tuned LLM approach outputs structured trading strategies specifying action type, share quantity, take-profit price, and stop-loss price, with a reward function covering path-dependent P&L, risk exposure relative to stop-loss levels, position-sizing appropriateness, and regulatory adherence — [digital.lib.washington.edu: RL + LLM trading framework](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/afe9230b-c33b-43f0-81fc-fcd9d33d95ab)
- A recent evaluation paper on LLM-based trading agents explicitly states that integrating reinforcement learning is "the most impactful next step," and separately flags a full-fine-tuning-vs-LoRA/QLoRA comparison for trading agents as valuable but unaddressed future work — i.e., the field has not yet published a controlled comparison for this exact decision — [AI Trading: Evaluating LLMs for Technical Market Analysis, arXiv 2607.15414](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.15414)

### Inferences
- Trading-R1's choice of a small, discretized action space (five actions) rather than continuous price prediction is architecturally close to vyapaar's closed Decision enum, which supports the general design choice of discretizing LLM output into a small closed set for trading decisions — but Trading-R1 uses a 100K-sample SFT+RL pipeline at a much larger scale than what vyapaar's narrow, single-enum task likely requires, since Trading-R1 appears to be training general market-reasoning capability rather than adapting to one fixed internal schema plus an external deterministic risk clamp.
- Because vyapaar already has a deterministic, non-bypassable risk-clamping engine downstream (per the project's own architecture) and treats the LLM strictly as a candidate-signal generator, the RL-with-trading-reward approach used by Trading-R1 and similar papers is less directly necessary — a SFT-only (or SFT + constrained decoding) approach focused on getting the candidate signal's distribution close to a human/teacher-labeled gold standard is more aligned with vyapaar's existing safety architecture than reproducing a full RL trading-reward pipeline, though no source directly validates this simpler approach for production use.

### Gaps
- No practitioner writeup or paper was found describing fine-tuning methodology specifically for a closed-enum trading-decision classifier that is explicitly *not* trusted directly and is passed through a separate deterministic risk engine (vyapaar's exact architecture) — this is a genuine literature gap, not just an unconfirmed claim.
- No concrete VRAM/data-scale numbers specific to financial-domain fine-tuning (as opposed to general tool-calling/structured-output numbers reported elsewhere in this document) were found.
