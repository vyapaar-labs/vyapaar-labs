# Local small models can replace cloud calls — selectively

A single fine-tuned local model cannot responsibly cover all four `vyapaar-agent` workloads, but a **tiered stack built on the Qwen3 family (Apache 2.0)** can, running entirely on consumer hardware from 8GB laptop GPUs up to a 24GB desktop card. The closed-grammar trading-decision parser is the easiest workload to de-risk locally because it is *already* architected as an untrusted candidate signal clamped by a deterministic risk engine — this means the real safety net is grammar-constrained decoding (llama.cpp GBNF, vLLM/Outlines structured output) rather than the model itself, with fine-tuning (QLoRA, a few hundred to ~1,000 curated examples) only needed to sharpen *which* enum value gets picked, since constrained decoding guarantees syntax but not semantics ([SLOT paper, arXiv 2505.04016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04016)). The tool-calling assistant and research loop need a larger, natively agentic model (Qwen3-8B/14B or Mistral Nemo 12B) and meaningfully more data (low thousands of examples) because tool-calling fine-tunes are documented at a much larger scale ([Nebius 10K-subset tutorial](https://nebius.com/blog/posts/fine-tuning-llms-with-nebius-ai-studio)), and quantization specifically degrades *which* tool gets called and with what arguments even when aggregate benchmark scores look flat ([NeurIPS 2026 poster 171153](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/171153); [arXiv:2607.27275](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27275)). Most consequentially for the SHA-256 replay-hash requirement: **none of llama.cpp, Ollama, or vLLM guarantee byte-identical output from temperature=0 plus a fixed seed alone** — reproducibility is conditional on pinning hardware, engine version, thread/batch configuration, and (for vLLM) an explicit beta flag, which means the hash record must capture the full serving-stack fingerprint, not just the prompt and completion ([vLLM Reproducibility docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/usage/reproducibility.html); [Thinking Machines Lab](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)).

## Qwen3's Apache 2.0 license clears the AGPL question that Llama and Gemma don't

The licensing landscape sorts cleanly into two tiers once the AGPL-3.0 interaction is examined directly. **Qwen3 (0.6B–14B dense, plus MoE variants), SmolLM3-3B, Mistral Nemo 12B, Mistral Small 3 24B, IBM Granite 4.0 Nano, and Phi-4-mini (MIT)** are all standard OSI-approved permissive licenses — Apache 2.0 for the first five, MIT for Phi-4-mini — with no copyleft or network-use clause, so they sit alongside an AGPL-3.0 component elsewhere in the stack without any license-text conflict: the AGPL component's own source-disclosure obligations are unaffected by, and don't affect, permissively-licensed weights, which are a legally separate artifact ([Qwen/Qwen3-4B model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-4B); [microsoft/Phi-4-mini-instruct](https://huggingface.co/microsoft/Phi-4-mini-instruct); [Simon Willison on Mistral Small 3](https://simonwillison.net/2025/jan/30/mistral-small-3/)). The direction of compatibility also favors this combination: Apache 2.0 is described as one-way-compatible into an AGPL-licensed larger work, meaning Apache-licensed model weights and inference code can be folded into a product that also contains an AGPL-3.0 component without forcing anything to "become" AGPL ([dev.to: GPL AI models navigating license propagation](https://dev.to/greygoose/gpl-ai-models-navigating-license-propagation-161c)). **Llama 3.2 and Gemma 3**, by contrast, are nominally commercial-use-permitted but carry real contractual overhead that is independent of the AGPL question: Llama's Community License requires a "Built with Llama" notice, a retained attribution Notice file, "Llama" in the name of any distributed fine-tune, adherence to an incorporated Acceptable Use Policy, and a monthly-active-user threshold above which a separate license must be negotiated with Meta at its sole discretion ([Llama 3.2 Community License mirror](https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct/blob/main/LICENSE.txt)); Gemma's Terms of Use similarly require redistributing the use-restriction terms, marking modified files, and shipping Google's attribution Notice, with no trademark grant and a termination clause requiring deletion of all copies ([Gemma Terms of Use](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/terms?authuser=0)). Neither Llama's nor Gemma's license imposes copyleft in the GPL/AGPL sense, so there's no direct textual conflict — but minimizing legal surface area argues for avoiding them when Apache/MIT alternatives exist. One genuinely unsettled question sits outside any specific model's license text: whether AGPL's network-use clause could ever be read to extend to weights trained or served using AGPL-licensed tooling. Ultralytics has publicly taken the contested position that trained weights are a "transformation" of AGPL training code and therefore inherit AGPL obligations even when combined with non-AGPL inference code, while other community discussion treats weights as data rather than as a derivative work ([Ultralytics GitHub issue #2129](https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/issues/2129); [Roboflow: Why AGPL-3.0 Is a Risk for Computer Vision Teams](https://blog.roboflow.com/why-agpl-3-0-is-a-risk-for-computer-vision-teams/)). This is industry-wide and unresolved, not specific to Qwen/Llama/Gemma/Mistral — it depends on what training/serving code the project itself AGPL-licenses, not on the base model's license, and warrants a direct question to counsel rather than a model-selection decision.

Mapped onto the four workloads: for the **closed-grammar parser** (workload 1), model choice matters less than the decoding layer, but Qwen3-4B or SmolLM3-3B are the strongest Apache 2.0 starting points because both are natively trained for agentic/tool-call-style output, which should make them easier to align toward a fixed schema ([Qwen/Qwen3-4B model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-4B); [SmolLM3-3B model card](https://huggingface.co/HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM3-3B)). Gemma 3 is explicitly disqualified as a *sole* solution here regardless of license, because Google's own documentation states Gemma emits no tool-specific tokens and enforces no grammar on its own — the serving framework must detect compliance by pattern-matching the prompted format — a position echoed by Gemma team commentary that "no raw model" enforces grammar unaided ([Gemma function calling docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/capabilities/function-calling?hl=ja); [HN thread](https://hn.matthewblode.com/item/43451406)). For the **conversational tool-calling assistant** (workload 2), Qwen3-8B/14B and Mistral Nemo 12B / Mistral Small 3 24B are the best-documented, fully-permissive native tool-callers, and the practical convention to standardize on across a Rust serving stack is the Hermes-style tool-call parser (`--enable-auto-tool-choice --tool-call-parser=hermes`), since Qwen3's own "native" parser mode has at least one reported vLLM failure (empty arguments) where the Hermes-format path worked ([Reddit r/OpenWebUI via mirror](https://redlib.hbubli.cc/r/OpenWebUI/comments/1kv0oty/qwen34b_serve_with_vllm_native_tool_call_issue); [SmolLM3-3B model card](https://huggingface.co/HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM3-3B)). A specialized Hermes-4 variant on Qwen3-14B (unconfirmed license claim, needs a direct repo check) would be attractive for workload 2's structured-JSON needs if verified ([dev.co Hermes-4-14B listing](https://dev.co/ai/llms/hermes-4-14b)). For the **autonomous research loop** (workload 3), no model in this research specifically targets closed-enum trading decisions or walk-forward experiment design — the finance-specialized models found (Fin-R1 7B, Agentar-Fin-R1 8B/32B built on Qwen3, FinGPT, Trading-R1) are tuned for reasoning/report generation rather than fixed-schema output, and none had license terms confirmed in this research pass, so they should be treated as unverified candidates pending a direct license check rather than adopted outright ([Fin-R1 paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16252); [Agentar-Fin-R1 coverage](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16802v3)). The notable signal is that Agentar-Fin-R1 is itself built on Qwen3, reinforcing Qwen3 as the right foundation for in-house financial fine-tuning. For **report generation** (workload 4), any of the Qwen3/Mistral conversational candidates should suffice, since Typst/Markdown generation from structured metrics is a lower-difficulty, lower-precision-sensitivity text task than the other three.

## Grammar-constrained decoding does the syntax job; fine-tuning does the semantics job

The research converges on a specific division of labor between fine-tuning and constrained decoding that maps cleanly onto workload 1's architecture. Constrained/grammar-based decoding — GBNF in llama.cpp, Outlines, Guidance, or vLLM's XGrammar-backed structured output — works by masking invalid tokens' logits to negative infinity before sampling, which makes it structurally impossible to emit a non-conforming token ([theneuralbase: vLLM structured output reliability](https://theneuralbase.com/vllm/learn/intermediate/structured-output-reliability/)). This is mature enough in 2026 to all but guarantee the Decision enum will *parse*. What it does not guarantee is that the *value* chosen is correct: one 2026 practitioner report found constrained decoding cut post-processing errors from 32% to 0.4% but, on the same test set, scored 91.37% accuracy versus 93.63% for free-form parsing, including a concrete case where constrained decoding confidently returned 1 instead of the correct 0.46 ([tianpan.co: grammar-constrained generation output reliability](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/16/grammar-constrained-generation-output-reliability)). A RANLP 2025 study adds a mechanism for why: instruction-tuned models (unlike base models) often show measurable *performance degradation* under grammar constraints, because the constraint forces the model away from its natural, highest-confidence output patterns into lower-confidence structured alternatives ([ACL Anthology, RANLP 2025](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2025.ranlp-1.124)). The strongest available evidence that fine-tuning and constrained decoding are complements, not substitutes, comes from the SLOT paper: a fine-tuned Mistral-7B combined with constrained decoding reached 99.5% schema accuracy and 94.0% content similarity, describing the pairing as a "powerful synergy" ([arXiv 2505.04016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04016)). For `vyapaar-agent`'s workload 1, this argues for fine-tuning the model on the exact closed schema specifically so its natural output distribution already aligns with the grammar instead of fighting it, rather than relying on a generic instruction-tuned base plus grammar alone.

Data requirements diverge sharply between the closed-schema task and the tool-calling task. For narrow, single-schema structured output, LIMA's result is the most relevant analogy even though it wasn't run on a closed-enum task directly: fine-tuning a 65B LLaMA on just 1,000 curated prompt-response pairs (750 sourced, 250 hand-written) produced outputs human raters preferred over GPT-4 in 43% of comparisons, supporting the "superficial alignment hypothesis" that fine-tuning mainly teaches *format and style* rather than new knowledge ([arXiv 2305.11206](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11206)). By extension, a few hundred to ~1,000 carefully curated examples covering the Decision enum's full space — including edge cases and class balance — plausibly suffices for workload 1, though no source gives a dataset-size figure for closed-enum classification specifically; this is an inference, not a cited number. Tool-calling fine-tunes for workload 2 operate at a different scale entirely: APIGen's verified function-calling dataset runs 60,000 entries across 3,673 APIs ([NeurIPS 2024 APIGen](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/61cce86d180b1184949e58939c4f983d-Abstract.html)), TinyAgent trained edge models on 80,000 examples ([arXiv 2409.00608](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.00608)), and ToolACE curates a 26,507-API pool ([ToolACE summary](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/5ac1829317c44a1fda91762a)) — but a practical Nebius tutorial found a 10K-example subset of ToolACE still captured the benefits of fine-tuning while controlling training cost ([Nebius fine-tuning tutorial](https://nebius.com/blog/posts/fine-tuning-llms-with-nebius-ai-studio)), and one vendor's practitioner guidance pegs 5–10 distinct tools as the "sweet spot" for a first function-calling run ([Ertas.ai](https://www.ertas.ai/blog/fine-tuning-tool-calling-ai-agents)) — since `vyapaar-agent`'s typed read-only tool registry likely has more tools than that sweet spot, low-to-mid thousands of examples is the realistic target rather than LIMA-scale hundreds.

On synthetic data: `vyapaar-agent`'s own architecture offers an unusually strong mitigation against the literature's biggest warning. The consistent pitfall across sources is that a student model trained via distillation can never exceed a teacher model's competence on tasks the teacher demonstrates poorly — teacher errors get amplified, not corrected, in the student ([PremAI synthetic data guide](https://www.premai.io/blog/how-to-generate-synthetic-training-data-for-llm-fine-tuning-2026-guide/)) — and a Microsoft distillation study separately warns against relying on LLM-judge grading alone, recommending it be combined with ground-truth accuracy benchmarks ([arXiv 2410.18588](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18588v1)). APIGen's own pipeline addresses this by running every synthetic example through three verification stages — format checking, actual execution, semantic verification — before training inclusion, rather than trusting the generator's output directly ([NeurIPS 2024 APIGen](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/61cce86d180b1184949e58939c4f983d-Abstract.html)). Because `vyapaar-agent` already has a deterministic risk-clamping engine and schema validator sitting downstream of the LLM, synthetic (market/portfolio context → Decision enum) examples can be run through that exact oracle before being accepted into the training set — closing the teacher-error-amplification gap with a ground-truth check the project already owns, rather than relying purely on a frontier-model teacher's self-consistency. The same execute-and-verify pattern applies to workload 2's synthetic tool-calling examples, verified by actually executing generated calls against a sandboxed version of the real read-only API.

## Quantization is cheap on memory and dangerous on tool-calling, specifically

Memory and speed on consumer hardware are not the binding constraint for any of the four workloads — the binding constraint is that quantization degrades structured output and tool-calling in ways that standard benchmarks actively hide. At **Q4_K_M**, weight footprints run roughly 1.8–2.5GB for 3B models, 4–5GB for 7B, and 8.5–10GB for 14B, fitting comfortably within every consumer GPU tier from 8GB up, and well inside 16–32GB of system RAM ([willitrunai.com](https://willitrunai.com/can-run/llama-3.2-3b-on-rtx-5000-ada-32gb); [GPU vendor RTX 4060 benchmarks](https://docs.bswen.com/blog/2026-03-27-rtx-4060-token-speed-benchmark-coding)). The most methodologically credible GPU speed figure found — because it states its context-length methodology explicitly — is `hardware-corner.net`'s measured llama.cpp throughput for a 14B model on an RTX 4080 (16GB): ~51.2 tok/s at 16K context, dropping to ~40.6 tok/s at 32K context ([hardware-corner.net RTX 4080 benchmarks](https://www.hardware-corner.net/gpu-llm-benchmarks/rtx-4080/)). CPU-only inference for 7B–14B models on laptop RAM has no credible measured benchmark in this research; only a theoretical memory-bandwidth ceiling suggests roughly 8 tok/s on DDR4 and 14 tok/s on DDR5 ([CPU-only LLM guide, 2026] — flagged theoretical, not measured). This is a real gap: for a CPU-only deployment tier, expect single-digit-to-low-teens tokens/second as a planning assumption, not a verified number.

The more consequential finding is that quantization's damage to structured output is largely invisible to the metrics teams normally watch. A τ²-bench multi-turn agent study found that task-score changes under 4-bit quantization stayed within ±7.5 points — looking essentially "free" on the headline metric — while per-call failure analysis showed 4-bit amplified existing tool-name-hallucination failures by up to 2.5x in volume; shrinking the benchmark's permitted error tolerance from ten errors to two revealed a 17-point accuracy gap the standard metric had been quietly absorbing ([arXiv:2607.27275](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27275)). A BFCL single-turn study similarly found up to 13.50% of individual per-call decisions changed between BF16 and INT4 even when aggregate accuracy stayed flat or improved, with argument-value changes ("permission drift") outnumbering tool-selection changes ([NeurIPS 2026 listing 173065](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/173065)). Most directly relevant to model sizing: a NeurIPS 2026 SLM-Agents workshop poster found Qwen3-1.7B's missed-required-tool-call rate nearly tripled under 4-bit NF4 quantization (6.0% → 17.3%, McNemar p=0.0001), while Qwen3-4B showed *no* measurable effect at the same bit-width — a statistically significant size-dependent interaction (p=0.006) that led the authors to conclude "4-bit safety should be validated per checkpoint instead of assumed from a size threshold" ([NeurIPS 2026 poster 171153](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/171153)). A separate Qwen3.8-27B benchmark found 4-bit variants "hold up well" for tool use, but recommended avoiding anything below Q3 for structured output, since 1-bit tool-call validity fell to around 36% ([Qwen3.8-27B quantization benchmark](https://dailyaiworld.com/blogs/qwen38-27b-quantization-benchmarks-bit-holds-up-bit)). The practical rule this supports for `vyapaar-agent`: **treat Q4_K_M as the usable floor, not a safe default** — validate structured-output and tool-calling reliability per checkpoint after quantizing, and prefer the smallest model only after confirming it doesn't fall into the small-model fragility zone the Qwen3-1.7B result demonstrates. Where available, an official quantization-aware-training (QAT) checkpoint — Google shipped one for the Gemma 4 family in mid-2026 — or a reputable community "dynamic" quant (Unsloth's Dynamic 2.0/3.0 lineage) is plausibly safer than a naive uniform GGUF quant at the same bit-width, though no source in this research directly measured QAT/Dynamic-quant improvement specifically on tool-calling reliability, only on general perplexity ([Unsloth Dynamic 3.0 coverage](https://zeli.app/en/story/49365443)).

For fine-tuning itself rather than inference, QLoRA is the practical default across the 3B–14B range: roughly 6–10GB VRAM for 3B, 8–14GB for 7B/8B, and 10–17GB for 14B depending on rank, batch size, and sequence length, making a 12GB card workable for 7B fine-tuning and 16GB+ a safer floor for 14B ([InsiderLLM LoRA guide](https://insiderllm.com/guides/lora-training-consumer-hardware/); [Spheron 2026 GPU guide](https://www.spheron.network/blog/gpu-vram-requirements-fine-tune-llm-2026/)). Full fine-tuning is realistic only at the 1B–3B tier and still needs 16–24GB+ of VRAM — comparable to or more than QLoRA needs for a 7B model — so QLoRA on a somewhat larger base model is the better quality-for-hardware tradeoff ([PyPI backpropagate docs](https://pypi.org/project/backpropagate/1.7.2/); [Jarvislabs GPU guide](https://jarvislabs.ai/ai-faqs/best-gpu-for-fine-tuning-llms)). Unsloth is the clearest framework choice for single-consumer-GPU efficiency, reported to complete an 8B QLoRA run in roughly 3.2 hours versus Axolotl's 5.8 hours under matched settings, and to fit Qwen3 30B-A3B MoE fine-tuning in 17.5GB VRAM at roughly 12x standard PyTorch speed ([TheAIEngineer Substack](https://theaiengineer.substack.com/p/unsloth-vs-axolotl-vs-llama-factory)).

## The SHA-256 replay hash needs the whole stack fingerprinted, not just the prompt

The single most important correction this research surfaces for `vyapaar-agent`'s deterministic-replay design is that **"same prompt + fixed seed + temperature=0 ⇒ byte-identical output" is not a safe assumption for llama.cpp, Ollama, or vLLM** — it holds only under a narrower, explicitly-pinned set of conditions, and all three engines have maintainer-acknowledged non-determinism even at temperature=0 with a fixed seed. vLLM's own documentation states plainly that it "does not guarantee reproducible results by default" and that even its recommended reproducibility settings "hold only on the same hardware and the same vLLM version" ([vLLM Reproducibility docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/usage/reproducibility.html)). The mechanism is well-characterized: Thinking Machines Lab ran the identical prompt 1,000 times at temperature=0 against vLLM serving a 235B model and got **80 unique completions**, diverging first at token 103 — not because GPU math is random, but because the batch of concurrent requests a given request lands in varies with server load, and that batch composition determines which reduction/parallelization strategy reduction-heavy kernels (RMSNorm, matmul, attention) use, which then rounds differently ([Thinking Machines Lab](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)). Applying their purpose-built batch-invariant kernels collapsed all 1,000 completions to identical output — proof the mechanism, once isolated, is fully fixable, but only with engine-specific engineering, not by seed-pinning alone.

llama.cpp has the analogous problem in CPU-only serving, documented across multiple maintainer-acknowledged GitHub issues: the same seed on the same machine produces different output purely as a function of thread count (`-t 14` vs `-t 15`), because ggml's tensor-op work splitting changes with thread count and floating-point accumulation then rounds differently ([llama.cpp issue #95](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/95); [issue #280](https://github.com/ggerganov/llama.cpp/issues/280)). One discussion thread found even `--threads 1` insufficient for multimodal prompts, requiring the additional `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1` to control a BLAS library's own internal threading — meaning full determinism requires pinning multiple independent threading knobs, not just llama.cpp's own flag ([llama.cpp discussion #22956](https://gh.r-mj.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/22956)). Since Ollama is built on llama.cpp's engine and its own documentation offers no stronger guarantee than "set seed for reproducible outputs" with no hardware/version caveats, the same thread-count risk is a plausible latent issue for Ollama, though no Ollama-specific GitHub issue reproducing it was found directly ([Ollama api.md](https://github.com/ollama/ollama/blob/main/docs/api.md); [makandra: Ollama reproducible results](https://makandracards.com/makandra/626358-ollama-generate-reproducible-results)). vLLM does ship a purpose-built, opt-in fix — `VLLM_BATCH_INVARIANT=1`, in beta as of its v0.32.0 docs — that uses deterministic kernels for attention and forces a fixed-order all-reduce; it is explicitly framed by vLLM itself for "framework and model debugging, reinforcement learning rollouts, and large-scale systems that need reproducible results," i.e., exactly the audit use case `vyapaar-agent` has, but it has a documented gap under tensor parallelism for certain hidden-size all-reduce byte sizes, and one secondary source (not confirmed by vLLM's own docs) warns that vLLM's async scheduling, on by default, may still defeat batch invariance through request-arrival-timing effects even with the flag set ([vLLM batch invariance docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/v0.32.0/features/batch_invariance/); [Spheron Network blog](https://www.spheron.network/blog/vllm-batch-invariance-why-temperature-0-isnt-deterministic/)). This mode costs real throughput — Thinking Machines Lab's own implementation measured roughly 1.6–2.1x slower serving under deterministic kernels versus default vLLM, which the authors call "not disastrous" but is not free ([Thinking Machines Lab](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)). A separate academic mitigation — running inference in full FP32 rather than BF16 — eliminated essentially all measured output divergence in one controlled study (Std@Acc 0 vs 0.0544 under BF16 on AIME'24), confirming numerical precision is itself an independent, addressable reproducibility lever, though it is not shipped as a toggle in any of the three engines and would need to be patched in ([arXiv:2506.09501v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09501v2)).

Two gaps matter specifically for `vyapaar-agent`'s architecture. First, no source in this research directly tested whether quantization level (Q4_K_M vs Q8_0, or INT4 vs INT8) changes determinism risk — the only precision-related evidence is BF16-vs-FP32, which suggests by extension (not direct measurement) that lower-bit quantization would carry equal-or-greater reduction-order sensitivity, but this is an inference, not a cited finding. Second, no source found a true scaling study isolating model size as a determinism variable; the one spot-check available (32B vs 7–8B) found the larger model exhibits the *same* reproducibility challenges, not materially better or worse ones ([arXiv:2506.09501v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09501v2)). Given all of this, the practical implication for the SHA-256 replay-hash design is direct: the hash record should capture engine binary/version, the exact model file hash (not a model-name tag), the quantization build, hardware identity, thread count and all BLAS/OMP threading environment variables for CPU paths, and — for any vLLM deployment — whether `VLLM_BATCH_INVARIANT=1` was set and whether async scheduling was disabled, alongside the prompt and completion. Treating "hardware+engine version+thread/batch config" as part of the provenance record, rather than assuming replay works purely from a fixed seed, is the only approach consistent with what all three engines' own documentation and GitHub issue histories actually demonstrate. For a single-user, single-request-at-a-time deployment — which matches `vyapaar-agent`'s likely usage pattern — llama.cpp's simpler non-continuous-batching execution model sidesteps vLLM's dominant non-determinism source entirely, at the cost of needing careful thread/BLAS pinning instead; this makes llama.cpp the structurally safer default for the specific replay-determinism requirement, with vLLM's batch-invariance mode as the fallback only if vLLM's throughput/batching advantages are needed for the research-loop or conversational workloads.

## A tiered deployment, not one model, fits the four workloads

Pulling the four analyses together, a single model covering all four workloads is possible in principle but a worse engineering bet than a tiered deployment, because the workloads have genuinely different hardware/precision/reliability requirements that a single model-and-quantization choice cannot optimize simultaneously. **Tier 1 — the closed-grammar decision parser** should run the smallest viable Apache 2.0 model (Qwen3-4B or SmolLM3-3B) at Q4_K_M or higher via llama.cpp's GBNF grammar support, fine-tuned with QLoRA on a few hundred to ~1,000 execute-and-verify-filtered examples; minimum viable hardware is an **8GB consumer GPU or a CPU-only laptop with 16GB RAM**, since this tier's low parameter count and the external deterministic risk clamp mean correctness risk is bounded regardless of raw model capability. **Tier 2 — the conversational tool-calling assistant** needs a larger, natively agentic model (Qwen3-8B/14B or Mistral Nemo 12B/Mistral Small 3 24B) served via vLLM with the Hermes-style tool-call parser, fine-tuned on low-to-mid thousands of execute-and-verified examples against the typed tool registry; given the demonstrated size-dependent quantization fragility (Qwen3-1.7B degrading sharply at 4-bit where Qwen3-4B did not), this tier should stay at Q4_K_M or above and get its tool-calling reliability validated per-checkpoint after quantization — minimum viable hardware is a **12–16GB consumer GPU** (e.g., RTX 4060 Ti 16GB, RTX 3060 12GB). **Tier 3 — the autonomous research loop**, with its open-ended hypothesis generation and longer reasoning chains, benefits most from the largest model the hardware budget allows (Qwen3-14B or Mistral Small 3 24B) and the most context headroom, since no finance-specialized open model was found that specifically targets this task shape; minimum viable hardware is a **24GB consumer GPU** (e.g., RTX 4090) for comfortable margin, or a 32GB+ unified-memory Apple Silicon machine, where llama.cpp's Metal backend remains competitive at 4-bit quantization despite MLX's reported edge at FP16 ([digitalapplied.com MLX guide](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/apple-mlx-framework-local-ai-developers-2026-guide)). **Tier 4 — Typst/Markdown report generation** is the least precision-sensitive workload and can reuse whichever Tier 2 or Tier 3 model is already loaded, needing no dedicated fine-tune.

The deeper takeaway is that `vyapaar-agent`'s existing architecture — treating the LLM as an untrusted candidate signal clamped by a separate deterministic engine — is precisely the right posture for all four workloads, not just workload 1, given what this research found: constrained decoding guarantees form but not content, quantization quietly degrades correctness in ways aggregate metrics hide, and no serving engine actually guarantees deterministic replay without careful, explicit pinning. None of this blocks local deployment on consumer hardware — the memory and throughput numbers are comfortable even at the 8GB tier for the easiest workload — but it does mean the project should budget engineering effort for per-checkpoint quantization validation and for extending the SHA-256 hash record to the full serving-stack fingerprint, rather than treating "local model + fixed seed" as a solved reproducibility problem.
